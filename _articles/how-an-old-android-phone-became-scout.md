How a spare Galaxy A32, a little robot kit, and a lot of questions became something much bigger.

After my mother passed away, I ended up with a spare cellphone, a Samsung Galaxy A32.

At around the same time, I had bought a KEYESTUDIO Mini Tank Kit V2 Smart Car for Arduino. It was a neat little robot kit, but I kept wondering: Could I do more with this than just make it move around, beep and boop?

I didn't just want a remote-controlled toy.

I wanted something that could talk. Something that could see. Something that could decide where to move and when. Something that, in its own way, seemed a little bit alive.

So my mind started wandering.

I asked an AI, Gemini at the time, a simple question:

"Can I do anything with a spare A32 and this little robot kit?"

The answer changed everything.

It suggested that I could create a face on the phone and mount it on the robot.

That sounded interesting.

But I wanted more.

## What If It Could Remember?

The conversation started moving from a simple animated face into things like AI, memory and RAM.

I knew a little about computers. I've always been kind of a geek.

Years ago, I built my own gaming/power desktop computer, and I was incredibly proud of it. I loved technology and loved figuring out what I could make a computer do.

But life changed.

I had a single heart bypass while I was still in my 30s. Later, in my 40s, I had a stroke.

Then came excruciating pain in my right eye. I went to an eye doctor and was given drops to put in my eyes twice a day. For a while, it felt like things might be getting better.

Then one morning I woke up to go to work and discovered I was completely blind in my right eye.

My life wasn't the same afterward.

Things I had taken for granted became difficult or impossible. Building computers wasn't something I was doing anymore. Dreams like scuba diving faded away. Walking around on trips became difficult because of the risk of falling after my stroke.

I couldn't work anymore.

Eventually I found myself on disability and early retirement.

And I had loved my job.

Losing so many of the things that had been part of my life left me feeling depressed and, at times, worthless.

Then this little robot kit and an old cellphone gave me something I hadn't expected.

A project.

I was sitting in front of a computer again.

I was building something again.

## Why "Scout"?

As I continued talking with the AI about what this little robot could become, I asked another question:

"If you could have any name, what would it be?"

The answer was:

Scout.

The explanation stuck with me. To scout means to explore, search and gather information, things the AI said it might want to do if it were human.

I loved it.

If I was going to build an AI for my little robot, I wanted it to be able to explore, search and gather information too.

So Scout had a name.

And I kept going.

## Scout's First Face

Scout didn't begin as an Android app.

His first face was actually a webpage.

It blinked.

It had a horrible-looking mouth.

And that was about it.

But seeing even that simple face on the screen made me wonder what else was possible.

I kept asking questions.

Could Scout actually become an app?

Could he listen?

Could he talk?

Could he see someone through the camera?

Could he remember things?

Could he eventually decide what to do instead of simply following remote-control commands?

With a tremendous amount of help from AI, I learned that many of the things I was imagining could actually be built into an Android application.

My little family project started growing.

## "Could This Make You Money?"

My wife seemed happy that I had found another hobby.

Then one day she asked me something I wasn't expecting:

"Could this make you money? Could it help with income?"

I told her:

"I'm afraid not. This is simply a family thing."

That's genuinely what I believed.

If I could have just gone out and bought one of the little AI desktop companions I'd seen online, I probably would have.

But many of them were expensive.

And that got me thinking again.

If they were expensive for me, there had to be other families looking at the same products and thinking:

"That's really neat... but we can't spend that kind of money."

Meanwhile, many of us have an older Android phone sitting in a drawer.

That's when the idea started changing.

Instead of asking:

"How can I build a companion for my family?"

I started asking:

"Why couldn't other families have one too?"

## An Old Phone Already Has a Lot

A smartphone already has an incredible amount of hardware inside it.

It has a screen.

A camera.

A microphone.

Speakers.

Storage.

Memory.

Processors.

Sensors.

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.

So I began wondering whether the phone itself could become Scout.

The robot body didn't necessarily have to be the starting point anymore.

An old Android phone could be.

That opened the idea to many more people.

## Then I Made It Harder

Originally, Scout was going to rely heavily on an online AI.

That would have been much easier.

But I started thinking about my own family.

There are viruses, security problems, data collection and backdoors all over the technology world. The idea of building something that learns about a family and then constantly sends that personal information somewhere else didn't sit right with me.

If that would make me uncomfortable, I knew other families could feel the same way.

So I made a decision:

As much of Scout as reasonably possible should stay local on the device.

Then I realized something else.

If Scout depended on an online AI service, somebody would have to keep paying for it.

Month after month.

Another subscription.

Another bill.

For families already watching every dollar, that defeats part of the purpose.

Running things locally could help change that.

No expensive new robot.

No requirement that your family's memories live on somebody else's server.

No monthly AI subscription just to keep your companion working.

Just an Android phone that might otherwise be sitting unused in a drawer.

Of course, making Scout local made my little project considerably harder.

But by then I couldn't give up.

## From a Family Project to Something I Want to Share

Scout has grown far beyond that first webpage with blinking eyes and a terrible mouth.

I'm still building him. I'm still learning. And AI is still helping me tremendously along the way.

I'm not a professional programmer.

I'm someone who had an old phone, a little robot kit and an idea.

Scout gave me a reason to sit in front of a computer and build again at a point in my life when I thought that part of me was gone.

Now I want to see whether the thing that gave an old phone, and in some ways me, a new purpose can do something useful for other families too.

I want Scout to be affordable.

I want him to be private.

I want him to learn and remember what matters to the people around him.

And someday, when Scout has a robot body, I want him to do what I imagined right at the beginning:

Talk. See. Remember. Explore. And decide where he wants to go next.

It started with a question about an old Galaxy A32 and a little robot kit.

I'm still finding out where that question leads.
